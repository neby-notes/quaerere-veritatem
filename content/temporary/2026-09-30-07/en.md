## Introduction

It is said that an artist "creates", that a craftsman "fabricates", that a scientist "discovers". The single word hides distinct actions, and separating them requires making precise what "to create" means in the strict sense and what the human being actually does when it produces something new.

## 1. Seven distinct verbs

Between **creating** (*creare*: producing from nothing, *ex nihilo*), **making** (*facere*: producing from something, in the generic sense), **fabricating** (*fabricare*: constructing with materials), **producing** (*producere*: bringing forth into the light), **transforming** (*transformare*: changing the form of a matter), **inventing** (*invenire*: finding or devising a new form) and **discovering** (*discooperire*: uncovering what already existed hidden) there is a decisive difference. Only **creating** does not presuppose prior matter; all the other verbs describe operations upon something already given. Greek and Latin already separated *ποιεῖν* / *facere* (making) from *κτίζειν* / *creare* (creating).

## 2. Creation *ex nihilo*

The doctrine of creation from nothing has a biblical foundation and a patristic development. In **Genesis 1:1** we read: "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth"; the Hebrew verb **בָּרָא** (*bara*) is reserved in the Bible to God as subject, for men *asah* (make) and *yatsar* (form), but do not *bara*. **2 Maccabees 7:28** specifies that God made things "out of nothing" (ἐξ οὐκ ὄντων). The doctrine is developed against Gnosticism in **Saint Irenaeus** and **Tertullian** and is fixed in the Nicene Creed.

Saint Thomas Aquinas systematizes it in *Summa theologiae* I, q. 45. He states that **to create is to make something from nothing** (*creare est aliquid ex nihilo facere*, I q.45 a.1), that **creation is not a change** (*mutatio*) — because every change presupposes a subject that changes, whereas creation presupposes nothing and is rather the total emanation of being from the first principle — and that **creating belongs to God alone** (I q.45 a.5), for man cannot produce the *being* of things and only operates upon what already is.

## 3. The decisive distinction: *esse* and *forma*

The whole problem is resolved by separating two levels. **God creates the *esse*** — being itself, from nothing —, while **man produces the *forma*** — a new form in already given matter —. Man can produce new forms — a statue, a symphony or a poem — but cannot produce the **being** of anything. That is why human "creativity" is real and, at once, analogous: it participates in divine creating — producing novelty — but does not equal it — it does not produce being —.

Three assertions that are not equivalent should not be confused. It is **true** that "the artist does not create the being of things" and that "human creativity is real but not equivalent to divine creation"; it is **false**, on the other hand, that "the artist creates nothing", for that would deny the real novelty of form. Passing from "does not create *being*" to "does not create *anything*" is the most frequent error and arises from using a single word for the two levels.

## 4. Art imitates nature

The principle "**ars imitatur naturam**" comes from Aristotle (*Physics* II.2, 194a21: «ἡ τέχνη μιμεῖται τὴν φύσιν»), developed in *Physics* II.8, 199a: "if the art of shipbuilding were in the wood, it would produce by nature the same results". Nature and art share **finality**; they differ in that nature carries within itself its principle of movement, whereas art has it outside, in the mind of the artist.

It is necessary to indicate what "imitating" means here: art **does not copy the appearance** of nature, but **reproduces its operation** — tending toward an end through form —. The physician does not copy the appearance of health; he reproduces the operation by which the body recovers its balance.

Saint Thomas gives the reason for the principle in his commentary on *Physics* (*In II Physicorum*, lect. 4, n. 6):

> «*Eius autem quod ars imitatur naturam, ratio est, quia principium operationis artificialis cognitio est; omnis autem nostra cognitio est a rebus*.»

"The reason why art imitates nature is that the principle of artificial operation is knowledge; and all our knowledge comes from things."

That passage traces an exact bridge: art operates through **knowledge**, and knowledge proceeds **from things**. The resulting chain is **reality → knowledge → art**, with nature as reality in its dynamism ordered to an end. Saint Thomas further adds that nature itself is "divine art" (*natura est ars divina*), so that human art ultimately imitates the art of God — albeit only "insofar as it can" (*in quantum potest*), because its form is extrinsic.

## Conclusion

It can be safely asserted that only God creates being (*ex nihilo*), that man produces new forms in given matter, and that art imitates nature by reproducing its operation, not its appearance. It should not be asserted that man "creates" in the strict sense, nor that he "creates nothing" — he produces forms —.
