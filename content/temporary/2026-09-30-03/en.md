## Introduction

Alongside the Aristotelian idea of a natural desire to know that culminates in contemplation, ancient philosophy offers another figure of the search: the one that starts from acknowledged ignorance, examines one's own life, and lets itself be led by a desire for the true that transforms whoever pursues it.

## 1. Socrates: ignorance and examination

The Socratic search does not start from knowledge, but from the absence of knowledge: the **awareness of not knowing**, the *aporia*. In Plato's *Apology*, Socrates recounts that the oracle had declared him the wisest precisely because he acknowledged his ignorance (21d–23b).

The instrument of that search is the **elenchus**, the examination — of oneself and of others — that puts opinions to the test. The phrase that condenses this activity is that of *Apology* 38a5:

> «ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ» — "the unexamined life is not livable for man".

Two precisions matter here. The first is that *anexétastos* (ἀνεξέταστος) means "not subjected to examination", not "without reflection" in the abstract. The second is the consequence: for Socrates, to seek the truth is **to care for the soul** — *epimeleia tēs psychēs* — and therefore a constitutively ethical activity, not a neutral exercise. The search is not separable from the life one leads.

## 2. Plato: the desire for the true

Plato turns that activity into a doctrine of **desire** (*eros*) oriented toward the true. In *Symposium* and in *Phaedrus*, *eros* is the force that ascends from the sensible to the intelligible; in *Phaedrus* (246a–247e) the soul already contemplates the "plain of Truth". And knowing does not start from zero: in *Meno* (80d–86c) Plato proposes **anamnesis** — to know is to remember — so that the search is a movement of return, not an acquisition from nothing.

The central text is the **allegory of the cave** (*Republic* VII, 514a ff.), together with the **analogy of the sun** (*Republic* VI, 508e–509b). The Idea of the **Good** (τὸ ἀγαθόν) makes truth knowable and is "beyond essence" (ἐπέκεινα τῆς οὐσίας, 509b). True knowledge is described as **conversion** (*periagogē*, *Republic* 518c–d): a turning-around of the soul, a transformation of the knower, not a mere sum of contents.

Hence, in Plato, **truth and good** are articulated together: to know the true is, at once, to become good. The search for truth is not a theoretical end isolated from life.

## 3. Two figures of the search

Socrates and Plato offer the figure of the search as **process** — aporia, examination, *eros*, conversion, ascent —; Aristotle offers the figure of the **term** — possession, contemplation, *theoria*. The first is a movement that is not exhausted; the second, an arrival that culminates.

The two figures should not be merged. The Socratic-Platonic search is existential and ascending; the Aristotelian one, teleological and contemplative. In something essential, however, they agree against modern sensibility: for both, the search for truth is not indefinite curiosity or accumulation, but an orientation of the soul toward something that surpasses and transforms it.

## Conclusion

It can be safely asserted that Socrates tied the search for truth to the examination of one's own life and that Plato describes it as a desire oriented toward the Good that converts the knower. It should not be asserted that this figure is equivalent to the Aristotelian one of contemplation: one emphasizes the process; the other, the term.
