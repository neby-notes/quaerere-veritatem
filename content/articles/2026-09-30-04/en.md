## Introduction

The question "if reality is one, why do we divide it into disciplines?" contains a trap: it presupposes that the unity of reality should translate into a unity of knowledge. Showing why that inference does not hold requires separating what is united from what is divided.

## 1. Three senses of "unity"

The word "unity" designates here three different things that are usually confused: **ontological unity** (asserting that reality is one), **epistemological unity** (asserting that there is a single method for everything) and **organizational unity** (asserting that there is a single discipline encompassing everything). The first is a working metaphysical thesis; the second is false; the third, besides being false, is undesirable.

The logical point is that from ontological unity neither the epistemological nor the organizational **follows**. Reality can be one and yet be known by different methods and from different aspects. The reason is the finitude of the intellect: the real is known **successively and by aspects**, not all at once. Therefore, disciplines do not divide *reality* — which remains one — but **the mode of access** of a finite intellect to that reality. Disciplinary plurality is a fact of the *knowing subject*, not a fracture of the *known object*.

## 2. Historical genesis of the disciplines

Disciplinary organization has a history and is not a natural given.

**Aristotle** (*Metaphysics* E.1, 1025b–1026a) distinguished theoretical, practical and productive sciences, hierarchized and crowned by first philosophy — theology — as the science of the first causes; unity functioned as an architectonic ideal, for the sciences differed by object and method, but all were ordered to a supreme science. The **trivium** (grammar, rhetoric, dialectic) and the **quadrivium** (arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy) made up the seven liberal arts; the term *quadrivium* is attributed to Saint Boethius ("a sort of fourfold way"), and they were not loose subjects but **paths** ordered to a common end. The **medieval university** articulated four faculties — Arts, at the lower level, and Theology, Law and Medicine, at the higher level — and guaranteed the unity of knowledge through the **primacy of theology**: Saint Bonaventure, in *De reductione artium ad theologiam* ("The Reduction of the Arts to Theology", c. 1255), shows how all the arts are "led back" to theology as to their principle. The **scientific revolution** (16th–17th centuries) made physics autonomous with its own method; the **research university** (Humboldt model, 19th century) institutionalized specialization; and the fracture between sciences and humanities was described by **C. P. Snow** in *The Two Cultures* (1959). Finally, **transdisciplinarity** (Piaget, 1970; Nicolescu, *Charter* of 1994) proposes what lies "between, across and beyond" the disciplines, while warning — as Nicolescu does — against turning it into a "hyperdiscipline" that replaces them: "there is no transdisciplinarity without disciplinarity".

## 3. What justifies the division

The disciplinary division responds to two facts: the **plurality of objects** — or of aspects of an object — and the **plurality of methods** that those objects demand. Neither of these facts contradicts the unity of the real; rather they presuppose it, for the convergence of different methods upon the same world is a sign that there is *one* world approached from different sides.

It is therefore advisable to distinguish three unities that do not imply one another: the unity **of the object** (the real, legitimate), the unity **of method** (illegitimate) and the unity **of organization** (instrumental, not ontological). Only the first is a given. The other two are decisions that are not derived from the first.

## 4. Limits of the idea of "unity of knowledge"

The unity of knowledge is a **regulative ideal**, not a state possessed by a finite intellect. Two risks lie in wait for whoever absolutizes it.

The first is the **hyperdiscipline**: to claim a "science of sciences" encompassing everything would flatten the diversity of methods and objects and could become a new dogmatism. The second has a historical root: medieval unity was sustained by subordination to a supreme science, theology, and a modern unity cannot assume that form without deciding on a foundation that is today a matter of dispute.

Specialization is not, then, a defect to be abolished, but a condition of finite access to the real. What is indeed a defect consists in **turning the instrumental division into an ontological frontier**: in treating disciplines as sealed territories that forget they study the same reality.

## Conclusion

The division into disciplines does not contradict the unity of reality; it expresses it under the condition of finitude. Distinguishing ontological, epistemological and organizational unity makes it possible to affirm the unity of the real without denying the legitimate plurality of methods.
